

Climate Governance and DRRM Operations Guide for Philippine
## Barangay Officials
## 1 Executive Summary
Philippine barangay officials bear frontline responsibility for climate governance and disaster risk reduction un-
der three converging laws: RA 10121 (DRRM Act), RA 9729 (Climate Change Act), and RA 7160 (Local Government
Code). These statutes collectively mandate that every barangay establish a BDRRMC, conduct climate and dis-
aster risk assessments, develop local action plans, manage dedicated DRRM funds, and execute standardized
emergency operations—yet COA’s 2022 review found that only 56% of LGUs have conducted risk assessments
covering vulnerable sectors, just 33% have digitized risk maps, and two-thirds report difficulty programming and
budgeting DRRM funds [1]. This capacity gap is critical given that the Philippines ranks first globally on the World
Risk Index with a score of 46.56, with approximately 60% of its land area and 74% of its population exposed to
multiple hazards [
## 2,3].
Three findings are critical for closing this gap. First, the six-step CDRA methodology prescribed by CCC-HLURB
[4] and the nine-step LCCAP formulation process under DILG MC 2014-135 [5] are now directly linked to fiscal
incentives—LCCAP compliance is a criterion for the DILG Seal of Good Local Governance and a prerequisite for
accessing the ₱1 billion annual People’s Survival Fund [
6,7]. Second, the Mandanas ruling’s 23.9% increase in
nationaltaxallotmentsstartingFY2022[8]proportionallyraiseseachbarangay’smandatory5%LDRRMFalloca-
tion,creatingbothgreaterresourcesandheightenedCOAaccountabilityforthe70/30mitigation-QRFsplit[9,10].
Third, freely accessible hazard platforms—HazardHunterPH, MGB geohazard maps, and PHIVOLCS FaultFinder
[11,12,13]—provide the scientific data inputs barangay officials need for risk-informed land use and evacuation
planning, but require targeted training through OCD and MGB regional offices—present in all 17 administrative
regions [14,15]—to translate map outputs into actionable local decisions. The most immediate priority is build-
ing barangay-level technical capacity to operationalize these existing mandates, tools, and funding streams into
functioning DRRM systems before the next major disaster event.

3 Legal Framework: RA 10121, RA 9729, and RA 7160
Three interlocking laws establish the legal architecture for barangay-level disaster risk reduction and climate
governance in the Philippines. Understanding their provisions—and how they converge—is essential for every
barangay official.
3.1 RA 10121: Philippine DRRM Act of 2010
RA 10121 is the primary law governing disaster risk reduction. Section 12(a) mandates the creation of a BDRRMC
in every barangay, with at least two members representing civil society organizations, operating as a regular
committee of the BDC [9]. The BDRRMC formulates the Local DRRM Plan in coordination with local development
councils [
9]. The Local DRRM Fund (LDRRMF) must be sourced from not less than 5% of estimated revenue from
regular sources, with 30% set aside as a Quick Response Fund for relief and recovery [10]. Unexpended LDRRMF
reverts to a trust fund usable solely for DRRM activities within the next five years [
9]. When one barangay is
affected, the BDC takes the lead; when two or more barangays are affected, the City/Municipal DRRMC assumes
coordination [9].

Section 19 enumerates thirteen prohibited acts, including dereliction of duties leading to destruction or loss of
lives, preventing distribution of relief goods, and tampering with hazard monitoring equipment [9]. Violations
carry fines of ₱50,000 to ₱500,000, imprisonment of six months to one year, and perpetual disqualification from
public office for government officials [9].
3.2 RA 9729 / RA 10174: Climate Change Act and People’s Survival Fund
RA 10174 (2012) established the People’s Survival Fund (PSF) as a special fund in the National Treasury with a
minimumannualallocationof₱1billion,financingclimatechangeadaptationprogramsforLGUsandcommunity
organizations [22]. Eligible uses include water resources management, agriculture and fisheries, health, infras-
tructure, early warning systems, and institutional development for local governments [25]. LGUs are required to
formulate LCCAPs, which serve as the basis for accessing PSF grants [23]. Fund allocation is prioritized based on
risk and vulnerability level, community participation, poverty reduction potential, cost effectiveness, and gender-
responsiveness [
25]. The FY 2025 General Appropriations Act appropriates ₱1 billion for the PSF [26].
3.3 RA 7160: Local Government Code
RA 7160 provides the foundational governance framework. Section 384 designates the barangay as the primary
planning and implementing unit of government [
24]. Section 389 empowers the Punong Barangay to organize
and lead an emergency group during calamities [24]. The BDC (Section 109) mobilizes people’s participation,
prepares barangay development plans, and monitors program implementation [24]. Section 324(d) originally set
aside 5% of estimated revenue for calamity purposes—now superseded and expanded by RA 10121’s LDRRMF,
which covers pre-disaster risk reduction activities beyond post-disaster response [9].

4 Climate and Disaster Risk Assessment (CDRA)
The CDRA is a six-step, science-based methodology developed jointly by the CCC, HLURB (now DHSUD), and DILG
under the Supplemental Guidelines on Mainstreaming Climate and Disaster Risks in the CLUP [
4]. It serves as
the analytical foundation for the CLUP, CDP, and LCCAP, using the IPCC vulnerability framework:Vulnerability =
f(Exposure, Sensitivity, Adaptive Capacity)[27].

4.1 The Six-Step Process
Step 1—Collect and Organize Climate Change and Hazard Information.LGUs gather projected climate sce-
narios, hazard data (spatial extent, magnitude, intensity, frequency), historical damage records, and anecdotal
accounts from PAGASA, MGB, PHIVOLCS, and HazardHunterPH [4].
Step 2—Scope Potential Impacts.The team identifies areas covered by the geographic extent of hazards and
areas potentially affected by climate change impacts [4].
Step 3—Develop the Exposure Database.Data is compiled on exposed elements—population, urban areas,
production areas, and critical point and lifeline facilities—along with sensitivity and adaptive capacity indicators
## [4].
Step 4—Conduct Climate Change Vulnerability Assessment (CCVA).Vulnerability is assessed based on the
degree of exposure (presence of people, livelihoods, and assets in hazard-prone locations [27]), sensitivity (sus-
ceptibility based on income, gender, livelihood, and settlement location [27]), and adaptive capacity (ability to
adjust, including managerial ability, access to financial and technological resources, and institutional environ-
ment [27]). The CCC VRA Training Manual recommends a five-level rating scale (Very High to Very Low) across
sectors including coastal, health, agriculture, water, forest, and public infrastructure [27].
Step5—ConductDisasterRiskAssessment(DRA).Risks fromhazardsareassessed based on likelihood, inten-
sity, exposure, vulnerability, and adaptive capacity [
## 4].
Step 6—Summarize Findings.Priority decision areas are established, and spatial development challenges, im-
plications, and risk management options are identified following four concepts: risk avoidance, risk mitigation,
risk transfer, and risk retention [
## 4].

4.3 Integration with Local Plans
CDRA results feed into the CLUP at multiple entry points—from situational analysis through zoning and plan
implementation [4]. Key applications include adjusting projected land requirements given current risk levels [4],
analyzing land suitability by considering climate change and natural hazard impacts [4], and recommending zon-
ing regulations within hazard-prone areas [
4]. DHSUD MC No. 02, Series of 2019 requires LGUs to mainstream
CCA-DRR in CLUPs [28]. The CDRA also directly informs the LCCAP, which considers climate change impacts on
the most vulnerable communities, ecosystems, and resources [29].

5 Local Climate Change Action Plan (LCCAP) Development
The LCCAP is a mandatory planning instrument under RA 9729 Section 14, which designates LGUs as“frontline
agenciesintheformulation,planningandimplementationofclimatechangeactionplansintheirrespectiveareas”
[32]. The DILG-LGA Enhanced Guidebook (2017) prescribes a structured nine-step process organized across four
modules [33].
## 5.1 Formulation Process
ModuleA(GettingReady)coversorganizingtheLCCAPCoreTeamandengagingstakeholders. Theteamshould
include professionals from territorial planning, disaster risk reduction, civil engineering, and local economic de-
velopment,alongwithCSOs,academe,andprivatesectorrepresentatives[33]. RA9729Section14mandatesthat
“barangays shall be directly involved with municipal and city governments in prioritizing climate change issues”
## [32].
ModuleB(Assessment)encompasses the CDRA—the nationally recognized standard tool for assessing locality-
level climate and disaster risks—and the Greenhouse Gas Inventory (GHGi) for ascertaining carbon footprints
## [33].
ModuleC(Planning)involvesreviewinggoals,identifyingadaptationandmitigationoptions,prioritizingthrough
multi-criteria or cost-benefit analysis, and integrating PPAs into the Local Development Investment Program
(LDIP) and Annual Investment Program (AIP) for budgetary allocation [
## 33].
ModuleD(M&E)establishesmonitoring, evaluation, andperiodicplanadjustmentalignedwithCDP/CLUPcycles
## [33].
5.2 Required LCCAP Sections
The LCCAP must contain five sections per DILG MC 2014-135: (1) Background—LGU profile, development vision,
LCCAP timeframe; (2) Climate Information and Situational Analysis—vulnerabilities, risks, GHG emissions; (3) Ob-
jectives—adaptation and mitigation goals aligned with CLUP/CDP; (4) PPAs and Policy Requirements—project
briefs with indicative costs, LDIP/AIP integration with Climate Change Expenditure Tagging (CCET); and (5) Moni-
toring and Evaluation [
## 33].
5.3 NCCAP Alignment and Compliance
LCCAPsmustalignwiththeNCCAP’ssevenstrategicpriorities: foodsecurity,watersufficiency,ecologicalanden-
vironmental stability, human security, climate-smart industries and services, sustainable energy, and knowledge
and capacity development [34]. The LCCAP must be consistent with both the NCCAP and the National Framework
Strategy on Climate Change (NFSCC) [
## 33].
Upon adoption by the Sanggunian, LGUs must furnish the CCC with copies within one month [32] and regularly
update plans to reflect changing conditions [32]. LCCAP compliance is linked to the DILG Seal of Good Local
Governance (SGLG) under the Disaster Preparedness assessment area [6]. LCCAPs grew from 137 in 2015 to
1,267 in 2019, indicating increasing compliance [35].
5.4 Funding and Support Mechanisms
LGUsare“expresslyauthorizedtoappropriateandusetheamountfromitsInternalRevenueAllotmentnecessary
to implement said local plan effectively”[
32]. The People’s Survival Fund (PSF), with at least ₱1 billion annually,
finances climate change adaptation projects, with priority given to LGUs that have enhanced CLUP, CDP, CDRA,
and LCCAP [7]. The CCET under JMC 2015-01 supports mainstreaming of climate change into LGU budgets and
provides the starting point for measuring implementation progress [
36]. Provincial governments are mandated
to provide technical assistance in support of municipal and city climate change action plans [32].

6.1 HazardHunterPH: The One-Stop Multi-Hazard Platform
HazardHunterPH is a web and mobile application developed under the GeoRiskPH Initiative, led by DOST-
PHIVOLCS, that generates indicative hazard assessment reports for any user-specified location in the Philippines
[11]. The basic version is publicly available at hazardhunter.georisk.gov.ph without credentials, while Hazard-
HunterPH Pro (supporting multi-site assessment and CSV uploads) requires a GeoRiskPH account obtained
through an MOA and training [37]. The platform integrates data from three agencies: DOST-PHIVOLCS (seismic
and volcanic hazards), DOST-PAGASA (storm surge and severe wind), and DENR-MGB (flood and rain-induced
landslide) [11].
Each assessment report classifies locations as“Safe,”“Prone,”or provides distance-based assessments with
specific recommendations—for example, ground rupture assessments state the distance to the nearest active
fault with a recommended 5-meter buffer zone on both sides [38]. The EMB has adopted HazardHunterPH for
generating initial hazard assessment reports as part of the Philippine Environmental Impact Statement System
## [39].

6.4 Practical Application: Evacuation and Land Use Planning
Effectiveevacuationplanningrequiresoverlayingmultiplehazardlayerstoidentifyroutesandzonessafefromall
relevant hazards. Community mapping workshops that integrate local knowledge with scientific hazard mapping

plan implementation, monitoring and evaluation [
4]. Geohazard maps are useful tools for LGUs in formulating
their CLUPs and DRRM plans as these maps indicate the susceptibilities of the LGUs to rain-induced landslides
and floods, and coastal flooding and erosion [43]. MGB’s completion of 1:10,000-scale maps for all 1,640 cities
and municipalities nationwide [
12] means that every LGU has access to detailed geohazard data for integration
into comprehensive land use plans.

7 BDRRMC Organization and DRRM Operations SOPs

Section 12(a) of RA 10121 mandates a BDRRMC in every barangay, responsible for“setting the direction, devel-
opment, implementation and coordination of disaster risk management programs within their territorial juris-
diction”[45]. The BDRRMC operates as a regular committee of the BDC [45], chaired by the Punong Barangay
perNDRRMC-DILG-DBM-CSCJMCNo.2014-1[46]. AtleasttwoCSOrepresentativesfromactivecommunity-based
organizations representing the most vulnerable and marginalized groups must participate [45].
The committee is organized with staff responsible for three functional areas: administration and training, re-
search and planning, and operations and warning [45]. The BDRRMC must assign a Focal Person per thematic
area—prevention/mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery/rehabilitation—to ensure all approved pro-
grams are implemented [
46]. Barangay Health Emergency Response Teams (BHERTs) must also be organized,
comprising an Executive Officer, Barangay Tanod, and two BHWs for every 5,000 persons [46].

7.3 During-Disaster Phase: Response Operations
Upon threat or impact, the BDRRMC activates the Barangay Operations Center for 24/7 operations and imple-
ments the Incident Command System (ICS), with the Punong Barangay serving as Incident Commander [46]. The
ICS provides a“practical and standard framework enabling local governments and response agencies to imple-
ment coordinated and efficient disaster management measures”[47]. The NDRRMC Emergency Preparedness
and Response (EPR) Protocols establish four alert levels—Standby (No to Very Low Risk), Alpha (Low Risk), Bravo
(Moderate Risk), and Charlie (High Risk)—with escalating actions from sending advisories to activating EOCs,
preemptive/forced evacuation, and mobilizing rapid deployment teams [48].
Evacuation centers must be managed per Camp Coordination and Camp Management protocols, including des-
ignated breastfeeding stations, child-friendly spaces, women-friendly spaces, WASH facilities, and separate re-
strooms for males, females, and persons with disabilities [46]. The BDRRMC must provide“factual and timely
informationandearlywarningtothecommunityforimmediateandsafeevacuation”andconductsearch,rescue,
and retrieval operations in coordination with authorities [46].
7.4 Post-Disaster Phase: Recovery and Reporting
Immediatelyafterimpact, the BDRRMC conducts assessment of the affected population, recording“thenumber,
names, age, and gender of those needing immediate assistance, especially vulnerable sectors”[46]. In part-
nership with the Municipal DRRMO, the committee assists in damage assessment, recovery, and rehabilitation,
and conducts consultations with vulnerable sectors for“immediate and sustainable solutions”[
## 46]. Situation
reports follow the NDRRMOC SOPG 2024 protocols, covering affected population, casualties, damaged houses,
agriculture and infrastructure damage, lifeline status, and pre-emptive evacuation data [49].

9.1 RDANA Methodology
TheRapidDamageAssessmentandNeedsAnalysis(RDANA)istheprimarypost-disasterassessmentmechanism,
comprising two components: Damage Assessment, which provides a“snapshot”of the disaster’s magnitude
answering“whathashappened?”; andNeedsAnalysis,whichdefinesthelevelandtypeofimmediateassistance
required answering“what needs to be done?”[
60]. Section 9 of RA 10121 mandates OCD to formulate standard
operating procedures for the deployment of rapid assessment teams and coordination at all levels [60].
Two standardized tools are used.FormNo.1captures visual and initial reports of damage and needs during the
rapid phase.
FormNo.2
provides a more detailed damage assessment that serves as the initial baseline data for
the Post-Disaster Needs Assessment (PDNA) [
60]. RDANA aims to identify the general overview of damage—na-
tureof disaster, extent of damage, secondary threats, impact on local response capacity—and to recommend the
most urgent relief needs, potential delivery methods, and whether detailed sectoral assessment or international
assistance is required [
## 60].
9.2 Reporting Timelines and Data Flow
The initial report must be submitted within 3 hours of receiving information about the incident, even if complete
documentation is not yet available [49]. Subsequent periodic reporting follows a structured schedule per the
NDRRMOC SOPG 2024: LDRRMOs submit to RDRRMC agencies and OCDROs at 10:00 AM and 10:00 PM; RDRRMC
agencies submit to OCDROs/RDRRMOCs at 12:00 NN and 12:00 MN; OCDROs submit to NDRRMOC at 6:00 AM
and 6:00 PM; and NDRRMOC issues public situation reports at 8:00 AM and 8:00 PM [
49]. Significant updates may
be submitted ahead of the prescribed schedule at the discretion of reporting agencies [49].

9.3 Three-Tier Assessment System
The NDRRMC-approved Rehabilitation and Recovery Planning Guide establishes three assessment tiers.RDANA
(led by OCD, mandatory) uses the ASEAN ERAT Initial Needs Assessment Checklist and covers initial damages and
immediatereliefrequirements.RaDE(ledbyNEDA,optional)isadesktopexerciseforrapidestimationofdamages
and losses.PDNA(led by OCD, mandatory upon declaration of state of calamity or presidential order) uses the
UNECLAC methodology and covers damages, losses, economic impacts, and rehabilitation/recovery needs from
both public and private sectors [
## 62].
RDANA feeds directly into PDNA: OCD bases its recommendations for conducting a PDNA on the effect of the
disaster as informed by the RDANA Report, particularly the casualties and extent of damages which have impli-
cations on rehabilitation and reconstruction [
62]. The PDNA document prepared by the LDRRMO is approved by
the LDRRMC, while the concerned RDRRMC reviews and validates LGU project proposals submitted to OCD for
NDRRMF funding [
62]. NEDA serves as Vice-Chair for Disaster Rehabilitation and Recovery, leading the Technical
Management Group at national and regional levels [62].
9.4 Barangay-Level Responsibilities
The BDRRMC is the frontline data collection unit. The BDC takes the lead when a single barangay is affected,
conducting initial damage assessment and reporting upward to the Municipal/City DRRMC [61]. Baseline data
at barangay level should include geographical disaggregation covering physical/environmental characteristics,
population and residence information, and number of houses by construction type and ownership [
62]. At the
locallevel,theLDRRMOfficeleadstheconductofPDNAwhenthedisasterisconfinedtoafewlocalities,withOCD
assisting upon request [62].
