Philippine Disaster Preparedness & Safety Knowledge Base for
## Residents
## 1 Executive Summary

This knowledge base provides Philippine residents with actionable, government-sourced guidance on preparing
for and surviving typhoons, floods, and storm surges. The Philippines ranks among the most disaster-exposed
countries in the world, with its archipelagic geography placing millions of residents in the direct path of tropical
cyclones, storm surges, and flooding each year. Typhoon Haiyan’s 5–6 meter surges that killed thousands in
2013 remain a stark reminder of the catastrophic potential of these hazards [1, 2].
Three findings are critical for residents. First, PAGASA’s five-level TCWS system provides 12–36 hours of advance
warning [3], and evacuation should begin  at  Signal #2 for coastal  and flood-prone communities —not Signal #3
or higher —especially in areas where storm surge exposure is extreme even at lower wind levels [4, 5]. Second,
no single consolidated evacuation center directory exists nationwide [6]; residents must proactively contact their
Barangay Hall for designated centers and use HazardHunterPH (hazardhunter.georisk.gov.ph) to assess location-
specific hazard exposure before disaster strikes [7, 8]. The passage of RA 12076 in December 2024 mandates
purpose-built  evacuation  centers  for  every  municipality,  but  implementation  is  ongoing  [9]. Third,  post-flood
health threats —particularly leptospirosis, which surged to over 3,000 cases in the first half of 2025 [10] —require
immediate consultation with health centers for prophylactic treatment after any floodwater exposure [11].
The most actionable step for every household is to prepare now: save the unified 911 emergency number [12],
maintain a three-day emergency supply kit, and confirm your barangay’s evacuation plan before the next typhoon
warning is issued.
2 Introduction and Scope

This knowledge base provides Filipino-accessible disaster preparedness guidance for all Philippine residents. The
Philippines  faces  amplified  disaster  risk  as  an  archipelago  situated  along  the  Pacific  typhoon  belt,  where  the
largest  and  hottest  pool  of  ocean  water  fuels  intense  super  typhoons  that  sustain  their  intensity  all  the  way  to
landfall [13]. Coastal communities across the country —particularly those along funnel-shaped bays and low-lying
river deltas —face concentrated and amplified storm surge risks [13].
This  guide  draws  exclusively  from  authoritative  Philippine  government  and  humanitarian  sources: PAGASA
(weather warnings and forecasts), NDRRMC (disaster coordination and protocols), DSWD (evacuation and relief
reporting), DILG (local government disaster operations), PSA (geographic reference data), DOST-PHIVOLCS/
HazardHunterPH (hazard mapping), and the Philippine Red Cross (emergency response). It covers typhoon
preparedness  by  TCWS  signal  level,  flood  safety  protocols,  storm  surge  warnings,  evacuation  center  access,
emergency  hotlines, weather  terminology  in  plain  language, and  the  Philippine  geographic  reference  system
that underpins all hazard data.
Who is this for? Household heads, barangay officials, community volunteers, and any resident who wants to
know what to do before, during, and after typhoons, floods, and storm surges.
3 Typhoon Preparedness by TCWS Level

PAGASA’s Tropical Cyclone Wind Signal (TCWS) system, in effect since 23 March 2022 [3], uses five escalating
levels  that  assign  wind  speed  thresholds,  warning  lead  times,  and  impact  descriptions  to  approaching  tropical
cyclones  [3]. Wind  signals  are  typically  hoisted  at  the  province  or  city  level,  though  they  can  be  issued  at  the
municipality level when conditions require [3].

3.2 Before a Typhoon
Regardless of signal level, residents should inspect and repair roofing, doors, and windows [15], tie down items
that may be blown away [15], trim overhanging tree branches [15], prepare an emergency go-bag with a fully
charged  phone  and  powerbank  [15],  and  elevate  belongings  in  flood-prone  areas  [15]. Stay  updated  on  PAGASA
bulletins and evacuate immediately when warned by the LGU or barangay [15].

3.3 Evacuation Triggers by Signal Level
At Signal #1, no general evacuation is required —monitor and prepare. At Signal #2, preemptive evacuation
begins  for  communities  in  flood-prone,  coastal,  and  landslide-prone  areas,  as  directed  by  LDRRMCs  [4]. From
Signal #3 through #5, forced or mandatory evacuation is triggered for all residents in the typhoon’s direct path
[16]. Preemptive evacuation should ideally occur at least 24 hours before expected impact [16]. Priority goes
to senior citizens, persons with disabilities, pregnant women, sick persons, and children [16]. Evacuees remain
under the care of social welfare offices until their areas are declared safe [16].

3.4 During and After a Typhoon
During: Stay calm and indoors [15], keep monitoring news updates [15], and maintain your go-bag within reach
[15]. If  evacuation  becomes  necessary  mid-storm,  turn  off  electricity,  close  gas  tanks,  and  lock  doors  before
leaving [15].
After: Wait for official confirmation that it is safe before going outside [15] or returning home if evacuated [15].
Have electrical lines inspected before use [15], and locate main circuit breakers and water valves —turn off utilities
if damage is suspected [17]. Check on children and address their emotional needs after the event [15].
For all coastal communities nationwide, preparation should begin early —coastal and low-lying barangays should
begin evacuation preparations at Signal #1 given storm surge exposure, and evacuate by Signal #2 as directed
by LDRRMCs [4]. The NDRRMC has directed LDRRMCs across the country to implement preemptive evacuations
in preparedness memoranda [4].
4 Storm Surge Warnings and Coastal Safety

PAGASA launched its dedicated Storm Surge Warning System on June 17, 2019, developed as part of the Nation-
wide Operational Assessment of Hazards (Project NOAH) initiative to prevent a repeat of the Haiyan disaster [18,
19]. The system uses two alert types: a Storm Surge Watch for moderate-to-high risk surges expected within
48 hours, and a Storm Surge Warning for high-risk surges expected within 24 hours [18]. Bulletins are updated
every 6 hours and include estimated surge heights by province and municipality [20].

## 4.2 Historical Storm Surge Impacts
The Philippines has experienced catastrophic storm surges that underscore the importance of these warnings.
During Typhoon Haiyan (Yolanda) on November 8, 2013, surges of 5–6 meters struck coastal areas, with flood-
ing  extending  up  to  a  kilometer  inland  [1, 21]. The  NDRRMC  final  situation  report  recorded  5,982  deaths,  27,022
injured, 1,779 missing, and 12.2 million people affected [2]. Typhoon Odette (Rai) in December 2021 also gen-
erated  significant  surges,  with  PAGASA  issuing  warnings  of  2.0–3.0  meters  for  multiple  municipalities  across  the
Visayas and Mindanao [22]. Key river systems across the country compound storm surge flooding when rainfall
and surge coincide [23]. These events demonstrate that any coastal community along funnel-shaped bays or
low-lying coastlines faces extreme risk.

4.3 Integration with TCWS and Evacuation
Storm surge warnings are issued as a separate but complementary product alongside Tropical Cyclone Bulletins
[20]. When TCWS #1 or higher is raised for any province, storm surge warnings are typically issued concurrently
for coastal areas [24]. Critically, significant surges can occur even at lower wind signal levels in vulnerable bays
due to coastal topography and bathymetry [5].
Under DILG’s OPLAN LISTO framework, the Mayor leads as Chairperson of the Local DRRM Council, with progres-
sive response actions triggered by NDRRMC advisories [25]. When PAGASA issues storm surge warnings of 2.1
meters or higher, evacuation is explicitly advised [20]. Residents can check their location-specific storm surge ex-
posure through HazardHunterPH (hazardhunter.georisk.gov.ph), a multi-agency platform integrating data from
PAGASA, PHIVOLCS, and DENR-MGB [26, 27].

lines, monsoons, and localized heavy rainfall. The NDRRMC and PAGASA operate a graduated warning and re-
sponse system that guides evacuation decisions at the local level.

## 5.2 Evacuation Triggers
The NDRRMC Operations Center operates under a graduated alert system —Blue Alert for routine monitoring
and Red Alert for heightened emergency operations during active severe weather [30]. Under RA 10121, local
chief executives have authority to order mandatory evacuation when disaster threats are assessed, with minimum
critical  preparedness  guidelines requiring  early  warning  systems  and  orderly  evacuation  procedures [31]. Local
DRRMOs activate contingency plans based on PAGASA warnings, constantly monitoring bulletins and activating
the appropriate alert level [32]. During STS Ulysses in November 2020, a total of 64,552 families (231,312 persons)
were pre-emptively evacuated across multiple regions [33].

5.3 Do’s and Don’ts During Flooding
Before: Stay informed of daily weather conditions from PAGASA [34]. Know your community’s flood warning
system and evacuation plan, and assign specific tasks to family members [34]. Keep a stock of food (good for at
least three days), a battery-operated radio, flashlight, cell phones, emergency cooking equipment, and a first aid
kit [34].
When warned: Store drinking water in containers, move belongings to upper levels, and transfer livestock to
higher ground [34]. If advised to evacuate, do so immediately —turn off the main electricity switch and gas valve,
and disconnect electrical appliances [34].
During: Stay  indoors and report  flooded areas to the Local DRRMC [34]. Do  not  attempt  to cross rivers  or  flowing
streams where water is above the knee, go swimming or boating in swollen rivers, drive through flooded areas,
or touch downed power lines [34].
After: Re-enter  your  house  with  caution  using  a  flashlight —not  lanterns  or  torches —and  be  alert  for  fire  hazards
like broken electric wires [34]. Do not eat food or drink water until checked for contamination, and do not turn on
the  main  switch  until  inspected  by  a  competent  electrician  [34]. Report  broken  utility  lines  to  appropriate  agencies
and consult health authorities for immunization requirements [34].

5.4 Post-Flood Health and Structural Safety
Leptospirosis is the primary post-flood health threat. The DOH recorded 3,037 leptospirosis cases during Jan-
uary–July  2025,  with  1,114  cases  occurring  within  one  week  after  the  rainy  season  began  [10]. The  DOH  rec-
ommends  consulting  the  nearest  health  center  immediately  after  wading  through  floodwaters,  asking  about
prophylactic  doxycycline,  avoiding  walking  through  floodwaters  when  possible,  and  covering  open  wounds  with
waterproof bandages [11]. Symptoms may appear 2–30 days after exposure [35].
Dengue risk also rises post-flood as stagnant water creates breeding grounds for mosquitoes; the DOH urges
tighter  disease  prevention  amid  leptospirosis  and  dengue  surges  [36]. General  waterborne  disease  prevention
includes  boiling  or  chemically  treating  all  drinking  water,  wearing  waterproof  protective  clothing  near  contami-
nated water, and keeping rodents away by storing food and trash in closed containers [35].
Structural assessment should follow NDRRMC guidelines: inspect foundations for cracks or erosion, check walls
and  ceilings  for  water  damage,  and  have  electrical  systems  professionally  inspected  before  use  [34]. DSWD
DROMIC  reports  categorize  house  damage  as “totally  damaged”or “partially  damaged”for  post-disaster  as-
sessment [37].
6   Evacuation Centers: Organization and Access
## 6.1 Legal Framework
Philippine evacuation centers operate under a multi-tiered system governed by RA 10121 (DRRM Act of 2010),
which mandates Local DRRM Councils (LDRRMCs) at the municipal/city level and Barangay DRRM Committees
(BDRRMCs) at the barangay level to identify and manage evacuation centers [38]. The landmark RA 12076 (Ligtas
Pinoy Centers Act), approved December 6, 2024, now mandates the establishment of purpose-built evacuation
centers  for  every  city  and  municipality  [9]. These  must  withstand  wind  speeds  of  at  least  300  kph  and  seismic
activity  of  at  least  8.0  magnitude  [9],  and  include  sleeping  quarters,  separate  shower/toilet  facilities  (1  per  20
persons), health care areas with isolation rooms, breastfeeding rooms, mental wellness spaces, recreation areas,
women and child-friendly spaces, rainwater harvesting, and standby power [9].
DILG MC No. 2018-88 acknowledges that the pervasive use of schools, covered courts, and other government
infrastructure not built for evacuation purposes remains a key challenge [39]. Under RA 10821, schools may only
be used as evacuation centers as a last resort [40]. The DSWD-DILG-DOH-DepEd JMC No. 02 s.2021 provides
comprehensive Camp Coordination and Camp Management (CCCM) guidelines covering activities before, during,
and after displacement [41].

6.3 How to Find Your Evacuation Center
No  single  consolidated  public  directory  of  all  evacuation  centers  exists  nationwide  [6]. However,  residents  can
locate their designated center through several channels. The most direct step is to contact your Barangay Hall
—each  barangay  is  required  to  maintain  a  contingency  plan  mapping  residents  to  specific  evacuation  centers
[7]. During active disasters, DSWD DROMIC (dromic.dswd.gov.ph) publishes situation reports listing all activated
evacuation centers by name, location, and number of sheltered families [44, 45]. The NDRRMC (ndrrmc.gov.ph)
provides complementary data on evacuation operations [46]. Residents can also use HazardHunterPH (haz-
ardhunter.georisk.gov.ph)  to  check  if  their  location  is  prone  to  hydro-meteorological  hazards  and  identify  safer
areas [47]. During typhoon warnings, municipal and barangay LGUs announce activated centers through official
pages,  public  address  systems,  and  SMS  blasts. The  Municipal/City  DRRM  Office  (MDRRMO/CDRRMO)  maintains
the master list of all designated evacuation centers with capacities and assigned barangays.

## 7.2 Reaching Help During Network Congestion
During disasters, voice networks often become congested. SMS/text messages require significantly less band-
width and are more likely to get through [52]. Key text hotlines include: PNP at 0917-847-5757 [50], DSWD
at 0918-912-2813 [50], and Coast Guard at 0917-724-3682 [50]. Regional OCD Viber lines may also work when
voice calls fail, as Viber uses data/internet connectivity [53]. Under RA 10639 (Free Mobile Disaster Alerts Act),
telecommunications companies are required to send free disaster alert messages directly to mobile subscribers
in affected areas [54].

## 8   Weather Terminology Glossary
Understanding  PAGASA  weather  terms  empowers  residents  to  act  quickly  when  warnings  are  issued. Below  are
plain-language explanations with Filipino translations.

8.1 Tropical Cyclone Wind Signals (Senyas ng Bagyo)
The TCWS system, in use since 23 March 2022, has five levels [3]. Signal #1 (Malakas na Hangin) means strong
winds of 39–61 km/h expected within 36 hours —minimal damage to makeshift structures [55]. Signal #2 (Malakas
na Hangin ng Bagyo) warns of gale-force winds of 62–88 km/h within 24 hours, with partial unroofing of old struc-
tures and local power outages [55]. Signal #3 (Napakalakas na Hangin) means storm-force winds of 89–117 km/
h within 18 hours, with considerable roof damage and widespread outages [3]. Signal #4 (Hangin ng Bagyong
Malakas) warns of typhoon-force winds of 171–220 km/h within 12 hours —up to 75% of light structures destroyed
[14]. Signal #5 (Hangin ng Sobrang Lakas na Bagyo) means super typhoon winds exceeding 220 km/h within 12
hours, with near-total destruction [14].

8.2 Rainfall Advisories (Babala sa Ulan)
PAGASA’s three-color system launched in 2012 follows the principle: “Yellow means awareness, Orange means
preparedness,  and  Red  means  emergency”[56]. Yellow (Dilaw)  indicates  7.5–15  mm/hr  rainfall  with  possible
flooding  in  low-lying  areas  [57]. Orange (Kahel)  indicates  15–30  mm/hr  with  flooding  more  likely —prepare  to
evacuate [57]. Red (Pula) indicates over 30 mm/hr with severe flooding expected —evacuate immediately [57].

8.3 Storm Surge Warnings (Babala sa Daluyong)
Issued during tropical cyclones for coastal communities. Surges of 1.0–2.0 meters cause minimal to moderate
coastal damage —cancel marine activities and move to higher ground [58]. Surges of 2.1–3.0 meters cause
significant damage and beach erosion —evacuation is advised [59]. Surges over 3 meters are catastrophic, as
demonstrated by Typhoon Haiyan’s 5–6 meter surges in Leyte [21].

8.4 Thunderstorm Advisories (Babala sa Kulog at Kidlat)
Short-term, localized warnings valid for 1–2 hours when moderate to heavy rain with lightning and strong winds
are expected in specific areas [60, 61]. Seek shelter indoors, avoid open fields, tall trees, and metal objects.

8.5 Heat Index Warnings (Damang Init)
The heat index combines temperature and humidity to show how hot it actually feels [62]. PAGASA classifies four
levels: Caution (27–32°C, fatigue possible), Extreme Caution (33–41°C, heat cramps/exhaustion may occur),
Danger (42–51°C, heat stroke possible), and Extreme Danger (52°C+, heat stroke highly likely —stay indoors)
[63]. Those most at risk include older adults, young children, pregnant individuals, and outdoor workers [63].

8.6 El Niño and La Niña (ENSO Bulletins)
El Niño (Mainit na Panahon sa Pasipiko) brings abnormally warm Pacific waters, increasing the risk of below-normal
rainfall and drought across the Philippines [64, 65]. As of September 2026, PAGASA warns of an intensifying Very
Strong El Niño expected to persist through the first half of 2027 [64]. La Niña (Malamig na Panahon sa Pasipiko)
brings cooler Pacific waters, increasing above-normal rainfall and more frequent tropical cyclone development in
the Philippine Area of Responsibility [66, 67].

9 Philippine Geographic Reference and Hazard Mapping

9.1 The PSGC System
The Philippine Standard Geographic Code (PSGC) is a 10-digit hierarchical coding scheme maintained by the
Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA), structured as RR PPP CC BBB —where the first 2 digits represent the region,
the next 3 the province, the following 2 the city/municipality, and the last 3 the barangay [69]. As of September
2025, the PSGC encompasses 18 regions, 82 provinces, 149 cities, 1,493 municipalities, and 42,011 barangays
[70]. The PSA updates the PSGC quarterly through the interagency Technical Working Group on Geographic Code
## (TWGGC) [71].
While PAGASA does not embed numeric PSGC codes in its public bulletins, the province and municipality names
used in warnings correspond directly to PSGC-coded administrative units, enabling NDRRMC, LGUs, and DSWD
to map warnings to specific areas for targeted disaster response [72]. PAGASA organizes regional forecasts by
PSGC-defined regions, with regional PRSDs covering their respective areas [61].

9.2 HazardHunterPH: Location-Specific Hazard Assessment
HazardHunterPH (hazardhunter.georisk.gov.ph)  is  a  multi-agency  geospatial  platform  led  by  DOST-PHIVOLCS,
integrating hazard data from PAGASA (storm surge and severe wind), PHIVOLCS (seismic and volcanic), and DENR-
MGB  (flood  and  rain-induced  landslide)  [8]. Users  can  search  any  address  or  click  on  the  map  to  generate  an
indicative  hazard  assessment  report  for  that  location  [8]. The  platform  also  supports  bulk  assessment  through
HazardHunterPH Pro, which accepts CSV file inputs with coordinates [27].

9.3 End-to-End Disaster Data Flow
The PSGC framework enables a coherent chain from hazard identification to response: PAGASA issues warnings
referencing PSGC-aligned provinces and municipalities, HazardHunterPH provides location-specific hazard expo-
sure data mapped to administrative boundaries, NDRRMC/DSWD aggregate impact data using the same PSGC
hierarchy for situation reports, and LGUs coordinate evacuation and relief at the barangay level using PSGC-coded
geographic units [8]. This alignment ensures that a weather warning for “#Leyte(Palompon, Villaba, Matalom)”
[61] can be traced through hazard maps, evacuation plans, and post-disaster damage reports using a single ge-
ographic reference system.

10 Conclusion and Key Takeaways

Disaster  preparedness  in  the  Philippines  depends  on  residents  understanding  warning  systems,  knowing  their
evacuation options, and acting early. The key actions every household should take:
Know your signals. PAGASA’s five-level TCWS system gives 12–36 hours of warning before typhoon impact [3].
Preemptive evacuation begins at Signal #2 for vulnerable communities, and mandatory evacuation at Signal #3
and above [4, 16]. For all coastal barangays, preparation should begin at Signal #1 given storm surge exposure
## [4].
Prepare before the season. Maintain an emergency go-bag, know your barangay’s designated evacuation
center, and save emergency hotlines —especially 911 (national emergency) and 143 (Red Cross) [12, 49]. Use
HazardHunterPH (hazardhunter.georisk.gov.ph) to check your home’s specific hazard exposure [8].
Act on rainfall warnings. Yellow means awareness, Orange means prepare, Red means evacuate [56]. Post-
flood,  consult  the  nearest  health  center  about  leptospirosis  prophylaxis  and  avoid  wading  through  floodwaters
## [11].
Stay connected. During network congestion, use  SMS —text PNP at  0917-847-5757, DSWD at 0918-912-2813,
or Coast Guard at 0917-724-3682 [50]. Monitor PAGASA bulletins at bagong.pagasa.dost.gov.ph and NDRRMC
updates at ndrrmc.gov.ph.
