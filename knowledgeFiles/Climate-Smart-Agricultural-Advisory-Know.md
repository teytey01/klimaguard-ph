## 1 Executive Summary
This knowledge base provides Filipino farmers nationwide with actionable guidance linking weather and climate
data to crop  management, pest control, livestock protection, and resilience-building strategies across rice, corn,
coconut, root crops, vegetables, and banana. Its release coincides with PAGASA’s declaration of a Strong El Niño
expected  to  intensify  to  Very  Strong  levels  by  late  2026  and  persist  through  mid-2027,  with  14  regions  already
reporting ₱6.87 billion in agricultural damage across 249,028 hectares [1, 2].
Three  findings  stand  out. First,  the  Philippines’four  PAGASA  climate  types  create  fundamentally  different  crop
calendars across regions—Type I areas (western Luzon, western  Visayas) have a pronounced dry season from
November to April, Type II areas (eastern-facing regions) have no distinct dry season with peak rainfall November–
January, Type III areas (Cagayan Valley) are relatively dry November–April, and Type IV areas (central and south-
ern  Mindanao)  receive  evenly  distributed  rainfall  year-round  [3]—making  climate-type-specific  advisory  content
essential  for  every  farming  region. Second, crop-climate  sensitivity  analysis  reveals  that  the  interaction  of  haz-
ard and growth stage drives the most severe losses: rice flowering above 35°C causes pollen sterility, corn yields
drop 40–90% when drought and heat coincide at tasseling, and banana plants snap at just 80 km/h winds during
bunching—thresholds that can be anticipated using PAGASA seasonal outlooks and ENSO bulletins. Third, a ro-
bust suite of climate-smart interventions already exists but remains underadopted: Alternate Wetting and Drying
reduces irrigation water use by up to 30% and methane emissions by 48% without yield penalty, PhilRice’s Sahod
Ulan  drought-tolerant  varieties  yield  3.2–3.8  t/ha  under  rainfed  conditions,  and  submergence-tolerant  lines  like
NSIC Rc194 survive 10 days of flooding—yet fewer than 10% of surveyed farmers practice crop diversification.
The  most  actionable  insight  is  that  farmers  across  all  regions  should  immediately  align  variety  selection  and  water
management with the intensifying El Niño by planting drought-tolerant or early-maturing varieties, adopting
AWD,  enrolling  in  PCIC  crop  insurance,  and  consulting  PAGASA’s  monthly  ENSO  advisories—while  extension
systems scale access to certified climate-resilient seeds through RCEF, which has distributed around 20 million
bags of certified seeds to over 2 million farmers across 78 provinces [4].
2 Introduction and Philippine Climate Context
This  knowledge  base  connects  weather  and  climate  data  to  actionable  farming  guidance  for  Filipino  farmers
across all regions of the Philippines. It covers crop calendars, climate vulnerability thresholds,  ENSO prepared-
ness,  pest  and  disease  management,  livestock  protection,  and  climate-smart  farming  practices  for  rice,  corn,
coconut, root crops, vegetables, and banana.
The Philippines’Four Climate Types. PAGASA classifies the Philippine archipelago into four climate types,
each with distinct implications for agriculture [3]. Type I (western Luzon, Ilocos, Central Luzon, western Visayas,
Palawan) has two pronounced seasons—dry from November to April and wet from May to October—supporting
two irrigated rice crops per year, with rainfed farming limited to the wet season [3]. Type II (eastern-facing ar-
eas including Eastern Visayas and parts of eastern Mindanao) has no distinct dry season, with peak rainfall from
November to January [5]. Type III (Cagayan Valley, parts of northern Mindanao) is relatively dry from Novem-
ber  to  April  but  without  a  pronounced  dry  season,  allowing  two  crops  with  irrigation  [3]. Type  IV  (central  and
southern Mindanao, including SOCCSKSARGEN and Davao) has more or less even rainfall distribution throughout
the year,  providing  the  most flexible  planting  windows [3]. These  climate  differences  mean  that  crop calendars,
pest  pressures,  and  climate risks  vary  significantly from  region to region,  making  climate-type-specific  guidance
essential.
Current ENSO Alert. As of September 2026, PAGASA has declared a Strong El Niño currently active in the Tropi-
cal Pacific, expected to intensify into a Very Strong El Niño between September and December 2026 and persist
through  the  first  half  of  2027  [6, 2]. Fourteen regions have already reported agricultural damage totaling ₱6.87
billion  across  249,028  hectares,  affecting  223,214  farmers  nationwide  [1]. The  affected  regions  span  the  entire
archipelago—from CAR and Cagayan Valley in the north to SOCCSKSARGEN and Caraga in the south [1]. Histor-
ically,  from  1995  to  2013,  the  agriculture  and  fisheries  sector  absorbed  27%  of  economic  impacts  from  natural
hazards, with typhoons accounting for 81.4% (₱188.47 billion) and drought for 13.1% (₱30.26 billion) of total losses
[7]. This knowledge base equips farmers and extension workers nationwide with the information needed to make
proactive, climate-informed decisions.

The  Philippines’four  PAGASA  climate  types  create  fundamentally  different  agricultural  calendars  across  the

## 3.1 Rice
Rice  is  grown  during  two  distinct  seasons  nationwide: the  dry  season  ( January  to  April  planting)  tends  to  produce
higher  yields  than  the  wet  season  ( July  to  October  planting)  [8]. According  to  FAO,  the  main  paddy  crop  is  planted
April–September  and  harvested  October–December,  while  the  secondary  crop  is  planted  from  October  with  har-
vesting in the first half of the following year [9]. PhilRice defines these as Semester 1 (planted September 16 to
March  15)  and  Semester  2  (planted  March  16  to  September  15)  [10]. Rainfed  areas  across  the  country  generally
support only a single wet-season crop, remaining fallow during the dry months [11]. In Type I regions such as
Ilocos, rainfed farmers plant rice only during the wet season, followed by vegetables and cash crops during the
dry season [12].

Type I regions benefit from a clearly defined dry season that supports reliable dry-season irrigated cropping, while
Type IV regions in Mindanao enjoy evenly distributed rainfall that allows more flexible planting windows [3]. Type
II regions such as Eastern Visayas face unique scheduling challenges due to persistent moisture and peak rainfall

## 3.2 Corn
Nationally,  the  main  corn  crop  follows  a  similar  calendar  to  rice—planted  April–September  and  harvested  by
September, with a second crop planted from October [9]. Major corn-producing regions include Cagayan Val-
ley and SOCCSKSARGEN, where two cropping seasons are standard. Corn follows a two-season pattern with wet
season cropping May–August and dry season cropping November–February [14].

3.3 Coconut, Sweet Potato, and Banana
Coconut is a perennial crop harvested year-round, with nuts collected every 45–60 days (approximately 6–8 har-
vests  per  year) [15]. Production  peaks  during the  drier  months (February–May)  when  nut  maturation  conditions
are  favorable. Major  producing  regions  are  concentrated  in  Mindanao—Davao,  Northern  Mindanao,  and  Zam-
boanga  Peninsula  lead  national  output  [16]. Type  IV  climate  zones  in  Mindanao  provide  the  most  consistent
year-round coconut production.
Sweet  potato  matures  in  90–150  days  [17]  and  is  typically  planted  at  the  onset  of  the  wet  season  (May–July)  with
harvest three to four months later [18]. It thrives with 750–1,000 mm annual rainfall at temperatures above 24°C
[19] and is among the diversification crops planted after rice in Type I climate areas [18]. Banana is a perennial
crop  harvested  year-round  approximately  10–14  months  after  planting  [20],  with  continuous  production  cycles.
The dominant producing regions—Davao, Northern Mindanao, and SOCCSKSARGEN—are all in Type IV climate
zones with evenly distributed rainfall supporting continuous production [16].
As of mid-2026, PAGASA reports that  El Niño is expected to intensify to very strong levels by October–Decem-
ber  2026 [21, 2],  which  may  shift  these  calendars  due  to  below-normal rainfall conditions—particularly  affecting
rainfed rice and second-season corn across all climate types.

## 4.1 Heat Stress
Rice  flowering  (anthesis)  is  the  single  most  heat-sensitive  stage  among  Philippine  crops. Temperatures  above
35°C  during  the  brief  pollination  window  (typically  10:00  AM–noon)  cause  pollen  sterility  and  failed  fertilization
[22, 29]. Corn  tasseling  and  silking  are  similarly  vulnerable,  with  pollen  desiccation  occurring  above  35°C  and
growth ceasing entirely at 38°C [24, 30]. Sweet potato storage root development declines above 30°C, with severe
yield reduction above 35°C as biomass shifts from roots to canopy [23]. Banana tolerates up to 38°C before leaf
scorching occurs [31], while coconut flowers abort under prolonged exposure above 38–40°C [25].

## 4.2 Drought
Rice requires 450–700 mm of water per cropping season, and yield declines sharply when soil moisture drops
below  field  capacity  for  more  than  5–7  consecutive  days  during  the  reproductive  stage  [32]. Corn  is  most  drought-
sensitive  during  tasseling-to-silking,  where  concurrent  drought  and  heat  can  reduce  yields  by  40–90%  [24, 33].
Coconut palms cannot survive more than three consecutive months with less than 50 mm rainfall per month, and
yield  depression  manifests  6–18  months  after  the  stress  event  due  to  the  long  flower-to-nut  maturation  cycle  [34].
Sweet potato requires adequate moisture especially during storage root initiation (5–8 weeks after planting) [23].

4.3 Flooding and Submergence
Standard rice varieties suffer severe damage after 4–7 days of complete submergence, but SUB1-gene tolerant
varieties (e.g., IR64-Sub1, PSB Rc68) can survive up to 14 days [35, 36]. Corn is highly intolerant—even 2–4 days of
saturated soil causes  significant root  damage [28].  Sweet  potato is  particularly susceptible to  waterlogging  after
storage root maturity, resulting in root rots and quality loss [23].

## 4.4 Wind Damage
Banana is the most wind-vulnerable crop: winds of approximately 80 km/h can snap ~90% of Cavendish plants and
destroy ~95% of bunches, especially during the bunching stage [37, 27]. Coconut palms tolerate strong winds but
not typhoon-force conditions—Typhoon Haiyan (2013) destroyed 33 million coconut trees nationwide with winds
exceeding  300  km/h  [38],  and  Typhoon  Rai  destroyed  over  10  million  coconut  trees  on  Leyte  Island  alone  [39].
Rice and corn are susceptible to lodging and stalk breakage at wind speeds above 60–80 km/h during grain filling
and tasseling, respectively.

5 El Niño and La Niña Impacts on Philippine Agriculture

ENSO events represent the single largest climate-driven threat to Philippine agriculture. From 1995 to 2013,
the  agriculture  and  fisheries  sector  absorbed  27%  of  economic  impacts  from  natural  hazards,  with  typhoons
accounting for 81.4% (₱188.47 billion) and drought for 13.1% (₱30.26 billion) of total losses [7].

## 5.1 Historical Damage
The 2015–2016 El Niño was devastating: paddy production fell 7.54% nationally (from 18.91 to 17.49 million tons),
planted  area  contracted  by  3.81%,  and  413,456  farming  households  required  support  across  16  of  18  regions
[40, 44]. During the 2023–2024 El Niño, prolonged drought accounted for ₱15.66 billion in losses. Tropical Storm
Kristine and Typhoon Leon wiped out ₱13.47 billion worth of crops, while Typhoons Nika, Ofel, and Pepito caused
an additional ₱9.9 billion in damage [41]. Cumulatively from 2011 to 2015, the sector lost ₱163 billion, including
2.9 million tons of rice and 1.02 million tons of corn [45].
The 14 regions reporting  damage  during the ongoing  2026  El Niño span the entire archipelago—from CAR and
Cagayan Valley in the north to Zamboanga Peninsula, Davao, SOCCSKSARGEN, and Caraga in the south [1]. As of
September 2026, 3,463 barangays nationwide are experiencing drought effects, affecting approximately 190,000
families [46]. During  La Niña phases, eastern-facing regions are particularly vulnerable to above-normal rainfall
and tropical cyclones that can lead to flash floods and landslides [47].

## 5.2 Farmer Action Checklists
During El Niño (drought), PhilRice urges farmers to plant drought-tolerant and early-maturing rice varieties such
as the Sahod Ulan series (NSIC Rc272–Rc348), PSB Rc14, PSB Rc68, and NSIC Rc222 [48, 49]. Alternate Wetting and
Drying (AWD) irrigation should be adopted to reduce water use [49]. In areas where water shortages prevent rice
planting,  the  DA  recommends  switching  to  munggo,  cassava,  or  other  drought-tolerant  crops  [50]. Additional
water-saving  technologies  include  low-cost  drip  irrigation,  rice  hull  gasifier-pump  systems,  and  windmill-pump
systems [49].
During La Niña (excess rainfall/flooding), PhilRice recommends submergence-tolerant varieties: NSIC Rc194
(Submarino 1) survives up to 10 days of complete submergence, PSB Rc18 (Ala) withstands 4 days, and PSB Rc68
(Sacobia) offers dual drought/flood tolerance [49]. FAO documented successful use of NSIC Rc194 in flood-prone
Bicol, where farmers previously experienced 40–80% crop losses during typhoon months [51]. Given that La Niña
increases the likelihood of above-normal rainfall and tropical cyclones that can lead to flash floods and landslides
in vulnerable areas [47], farmers should ensure proper drainage, clear canals before the wet season, and adjust
planting calendars to avoid peak typhoon months (October–December).

## 5.3 Government Assistance Programs
The  government  has  deployed  a  multi-layered  response  system. During the 2026 El Niño, the DA provided ₱10.96
billion in interventions including production support, 338 water pumps, and crop insurance indemnification [43],
with an additional ₱6.2 billion earmarked for affected farmers and fisherfolk [52]. A  Task  Force  El  Niño  was  estab-
lished focusing on water, food, and energy security [52]. DSWD provided ₱1.22 billion in emergency assistance
to 1.88 million affected families during the 2023–2024 event [53]. PCIC crop insurance covers natural disasters at
a premium rate of 10% of the sum insured, with maximum ceilings of ₱41,000/ha for self-financed farmers and
₱67,000/ha  for  borrowing  farmers [54]. FAO  also  provided  seeds  and  fertilizer to  approximately  64,300 farming
households in Central Luzon and Mindanao [40].
6   Pest and Disease Climate Triggers and Integrated Management
Climate and weather patterns are primary drivers of pest and disease outbreaks in Philippine agriculture. Un-
derstanding the specific conditions that trigger each threat enables farmers to anticipate problems and deploy
integrated pest management (IPM) before damage escalates.

6.1 Rice Pests and Diseases
Brown planthopper (BPH) thrives in rainfed and irrigated wetland environments with continuous submerged
conditions,  high  shade,  and  humidity  [55]. Closed  canopy,  dense  seeding,  excessive  nitrogen  use,  and  early-
season insecticide spraying that destroys natural enemies all favor BPH population buildup [55]. The DA Regional
Crop Protection Center in Western Visayas has warned that BPH becomes more difficult to control under high

Rice blast (Magnaporthe oryzae) is most favorable at temperatures around 28°C with high relative humidity (>90%),

Bacterial leaf blight (BLB) favors temperatures of 25–34°C with relative humidity above 70%, and is commonly
observed when strong winds and continuous heavy rains occur—making it particularly severe after typhoons and
flooding events [61]. Rice tungro disease, transmitted by the green leafhopper, is most frequently seen during
the vegetative phase, with plants most vulnerable at the tillering stage [62].

6.2 Corn and Coconut Threats
Fall armyworm (FAW), first reported on Philippine corn in June 2019 and on rice in May 2021 [63], thrives at 26–
30°C  with  the  life  cycle  completing  in  about  30  days  under  warm  conditions  [64, 65]. The  Philippines’tropical
climate allows year-round FAW breeding wherever host plants are available [66].

drought-affected areas [67, 68]. Major outbreaks occurred during 2010–2016 in Southern Tagalog and 2017–2020

## 7 Livestock Protection During Extreme Weather

The  Philippine  livestock  sector  accounts  for  nearly  30%  of  total  agricultural  output,  yet  climate-related  hazards
caused approximately ₱295 billion in total agricultural losses from 2011–2020,  with  typhoons  alone  responsible
for ₱262 billion (88%) [72]. Protecting  carabao,  cattle,  poultry,  and  swine  requires  phased  protocols  spanning
pre-event preparation, emergency response, and post-disaster recovery.

7.1 Pre-Typhoon Preparation
FAO  and  the  DA  have  established  anticipatory  action  protocols  where  communities  act  before  typhoon  landfall,
including  unconditional  cash  transfers  to  evacuate  assets  and  move  livestock  to  safety  [73]. Following  Typhoon
Haiyan,  World  Animal  Protection  trained  local  veterinarians  in  disaster  management  and  early  warning  systems,
enabling SMS-based typhoon preparedness warnings to small producers [74]. Farmers should assemble evacua-
tion  kits  with  7–10  days’worth  of  feed  and  water,  a  two-week  supply  of  medications,  restraints,  portable  livestock
panels,  first  aid  supplies,  and  copies  of  veterinary  records  [75]. Infrastructure  preparedness  includes  maintaining
backup generators, keeping vehicles fueled, and preparing emergency cash [75].

## 7.2 Emergency Shelter
World Animal Protection and the University of Aklan developed a typhoon-resistant livestock shelter model fea-
turing two elements: an underground shelter for storm protection (inspired by Cuba’s “vara-en-tierra”model)
and a modular shelter with a removable roof that three people can dismantle in under 30 minutes, leaving only
ground-level foundations that resist high winds [74]. The modular pig shelter holds 10–12 pigs and uses deep-
bed flooring with natural materials that decompose waste, reducing water needs and producing compost [74].
Philippine farmers have also adopted bamboo windbreaks around farm areas to weaken storm impacts [76]. Af-
ter  Haiyan,  livestock  smallholders  in  the  hardest-hit  areas  lost  20–30%  of  their  animals,  and  many  surviving  pigs
subsequently died from heat stress when pen roofs were destroyed [74].

## 7.3 Heat Stress Management
Hogs  are  highly  sensitive  to  heat  due  to  limited  sweating  ability—elevated  temperatures  reduce  feed  intake,  slow
growth, increase abortions, and raise mortality, particularly in backyard operations [77]. Broiler mortality rises
in  poorly  ventilated  facilities,  with  smallholder  losses  up  to  20%,  while  layer  chickens  experience  egg  production
declines  of  up  to  30%  [77]. Key  mitigation  strategies  include  shading,  ventilation,  evaporative  cooling,  nutritional
interventions  for  antioxidant  status,  and  genetic  selection  for  thermotolerance  [78]. Scenario  modeling  projects
Philippine  livestock  losses  of  ₱7.6–9.9  billion  under  severe  Super  El  Niño  conditions,  with  hogs  and  broilers  ac-
counting for the largest share [77].

7.4 Post-Disaster Recovery and Disease Risks
After typhoons, veterinary teams assess animals for respiratory disease, eye disease, and foot rot from prolonged
exposure  to  standing  water  [79]. World  Animal  Protection’s  post-Haiyan  response  deployed  mobile  veterinary
clinics providing antibiotics, deworming, vitamins, and vaccinations to more than 17,500 animals within five weeks
[74]. Leptospirosis remains the primary post-flood zoonotic risk—the DOH recorded 3,037 cases during January–
July 2025, a 43.4% rise over the same period in 2024 [80]. Outbreaks typically occur weeks after monsoon floods,
compounded by challenges in flood control, urban sanitation, and rodent management [80]. Weakened immune
responses from heat and nutritional stress may also worsen diseases such as PRRS and ASF [77]. PCIC provides
livestock insurance covering accidental death or disease for carabao, cattle, horse, swine, goat, sheep, and poultry,
with claims requiring notice within seven calendar days [81].

8 Climate-Adaptive Farming Practices and Resilient Varieties

Climate-smart  agriculture  (CSA)  techniques  offer  Philippine  smallholders  practical  tools  to  maintain  productivity
under increasingly variable weather. Three pillars—water management, stress-tolerant varieties, and diversifica-
tion—form the foundation of resilience for Filipino farmers nationwide.

8.1 Alternate Wetting and Drying (AWD)
AWD is the most impactful single practice for irrigated rice. The protocol begins two weeks after transplanting:
the field is allowed to dry until water drops to 15 cm below the soil surface (monitored using a simple field water
tube), then re-flooded to 3–5 cm depth. This cycle repeats throughout the season, except during flowering when
fields remain continuously flooded [83].

compared to continuous flooding, with reductions of up to 70% in controlled trials [86]. Critically, AWD does not
reduce yields—it may actually increase them through more effective tillering and stronger root growth [83, 87].

8.2 Stress-Tolerant Rice Varieties
PhilRice has developed an extensive portfolio of climate-resilient varieties. The Sahod Ulan (drought-tolerant)
series includes NSIC Rc272 through Rc478, with the latest releases yielding 3.2–3.8 t/ha under rainfed dry-seeded
conditions at 105–117 days after sowing [49, 89]. For dual adaptation, NSIC 2016 Rc480 (GSR 8) tolerates both
drought and saline conditions [89]. Submergence-tolerant varieties include NSIC Rc194 (Submarino 1), which
survives up to 10 days of complete submergence at the vegetative stage, and PSB Rc68 (Sacobia), which offers
both drought resistance and submergence tolerance [49]. For coastal areas, the Salinas series (NSIC Rc182–
Rc470) provides salinity tolerance with yields of 3.0–3.6 t/ha [89].

8.3 Crop Diversification and Soil Conservation
The Palayamanan system, developed by PhilRice, integrates rice with vegetables, poultry, livestock, and aqua-
culture  in  a  synergistic  model  that  increases  income  and  distributes  climate  risk  [90, 91]. However,  adoption
remains  low—less  than  10%  of  surveyed  farmers  diversified  production  in  both  seasons,  with  most  diversified
parcels being rainfed plots averaging 1.27 hectares [91].
For sloping lands, FAO established 129 SALT (Sloping Agricultural Land Technology) sites in Haiyan-affected
areas of Regions VI and VIII, enabling coconut-based communities to plant vegetables and cash crops using con-
tour farming methods [92]. Supporting technologies include protected cultivation (greenhouses, polytunnels) for
year-round  vegetable  production,  low-cost  drip  irrigation,  biochar  from  rice  hull  carbonization  for  soil  improve-
ment,  and  rice-duck  integration  systems  [91, 49]. During  drought,  the  DA  recommends  switching  from  rice  to
alternative crops including cassava, sweet potato, and munggo, which are more tolerant of heat and water stress

9 Institutional Programs and Farmer Support Systems

Multiple  Philippine  and  international  institutions  provide  technical,  financial,  and  informational  support  for
climate-resilient agriculture. Filipino farmers across all regions can access several overlapping programs.

## 9.1 National Government Programs
DA-AMIA (Adaptation and Mitigation Initiative in Agriculture), launched in 2013, is the DA’s flagship cli-
mate  change  program  encompassing  seven  systems-wide  mainstreaming  areas: climate  information  systems,
climate-resilient  agriculture  infrastructure,  agriculture  financing  and  risk  transfer  instruments,  and  a  climate-
resilient extension system. It uses 2,205 integrated climate change and multi-hazard maps and 41,995 databases
for strategic planning [93].
RCEF (Rice Competitiveness Enhancement Fund), established under the Rice Tariffication Law (RA 11203,
2019), distributes certified inbred rice seeds—including climate-resilient varieties—to Filipino rice farmers through
PhilRice [94]. From 2019 to 2024, the RCEF Seed Program distributed around 20 million bags of certified inbred
rice  seeds to  over  2  million farmers  across  78  provinces  nationwide [4]. The  amended law  (R.A.  12078,  signed
December 2024) extended RCEF to 2031 and tripled the annual budget from ₱10 billion to ₱30 billion, with ₱6
billion  allocated  for  rice  seed  development  and  ₱9  billion  for  farm  machinery  [95]. In  field  demonstrations,
farmers using RCEF-distributed certified seeds reported harvest increases from 3 to 6 t/ha [41].
PCIC crop insurance provides multi-risk coverage against natural disasters (typhoon, flood, drought, earthquake,
volcanic eruption), plant diseases, and pest infestations. The premium rate is 10% of the total sum insured, with
maximum ceilings of ₱41,000/ha for self-financed inbred rice farmers and ₱67,000/ha for borrowing farmers [54].

## 9.2 International Partnerships
The FAO-GCF Adapting Philippine Agriculture to Climate Change project is a $39.2 million, seven-year initiative
supported  by  a  $26.2  million  GCF  grant  and  $12.9  million  in  DA/PAGASA  co-financing,  directly  benefiting  1.25
million rural Filipino farmers across nine provinces in five regions (Regions 2, 5, 10, 12, and CAR) through climate-
resilient practices, awareness-raising, and enterprise capacity building [96, 97].
The IRRI EFFECT Project,  launched  in  September  2026  in  partnership  with  DA-BAR,  covers  multiple  regions  in-
cluding Ilocos Region, Cagayan Valley, and Eastern Visayas. It uses suitability mapping and innovation profiling
to match crops and technologies to local conditions [98]. The IRRI SuRE (Sustainable Rice Ecosystems) Project,
launched  in  July  2026,  operates  in  Tarlac  (Central  Luzon),  Camarines  Sur  (Bicol),  and  Iloilo  (Western  Visayas),  tar-
geting a 20% increase in rice yields and at least 30% income improvement through climate-smart technology

10   Conclusion and Priority Recommendations
This  knowledge  base  demonstrates  that  climate-informed  decision-making  can  significantly  reduce  agricultural
losses  for  Filipino  farmers  across  all  regions. The  Philippines’four  PAGASA  climate  types—each  with  distinct
rainfall  patterns  and  agricultural  implications  [3]—demand  region-specific  strategies  tailored  to  local  conditions
rather than one-size-fits-all approaches.
Immediate Priority: 2026–2027 Very Strong El Niño. PAGASA has declared a Strong El Niño expected to inten-
sify to Very Strong levels by September–December 2026 and persist through the first half of 2027 [6, 2]. With 14
regions already reporting damage totaling ₱6.87 billion across 249,028 hectares and affecting 223,214 farmers
[1],  Filipino  farmers  nationwide  should  act  now  by  planting  drought-tolerant  varieties  (Sahod  Ulan  series,  PSB
Rc14, PSB Rc68) [49], adopting AWD to conserve irrigation water [49, 84], and enrolling in PCIC crop insurance be-
fore the planting season [54]. In areas where water is insufficient for rice, switching to cassava, sweet potato, or
munggo is advisable [50]. The DA has already deployed ₱10.96 billion in nationwide interventions including pro-
duction support and water pumps [1], while DSWD has released over ₱714 million in aid to affected communities
## [46].
Building Long-Term Resilience. Beyond the immediate El Niño response, farmers should pursue crop diver-
sification through the  Palayamanan system [90], adopt SALT contour farming on sloping lands [92], and access
certified climate-resilient seeds through RCEF, which has distributed around 20 million bags of certified seeds to
over 2 million farmers across 78 provinces and now operates with a tripled annual budget of ₱30 billion through
2031 [4, 95]. The IRRI SuRE project in Tarlac, Camarines Sur, and Iloilo targets a 20% rice yield increase through
climate-smart technology packages [99], while the FAO-GCF Adapting Philippine Agriculture project provides a
$39.2 million framework for scaling these practices to 1.25 million farmers across five regions [96, 97].
Integrating Climate Information. Farmers and  extension workers should regularly consult PAGASA’s monthly
ENSO advisories [47], agroclimatic impact assessments [101], and PhilRice pest advisories to align planting deci-
sions, variety selection, and pest management with evolving weather conditions. The convergence of institutional
support from DA-AMIA, PhilRice, IRRI, FAO, and ACIAR creates an unprecedented opportunity to transform Philip-
pine smallholder agriculture from climate-vulnerable to climate-resilient.
